What this is about

An introduction to the two essays in this collection

There are two very different questions one can ask about the universe, and they are usually kept apart.

The first looks outward and backward, at the whole and its beginning: What state describes the universe? Why is it the way it is, and not otherwise? This question belongs to cosmology and to the physics of the elementary forces.

The second looks inward, at us: What can we actually know about this universe? Which traces of its history are still accessible to us today, and which are not? This question belongs to observation, to measurement, to the analysis of data.

At first glance the two have nothing to do with each other. One is about what is; the other about what we see. Yet the closer one looks, the clearer it becomes that they touch. For between what is and what we see there always lies the same thing: a place at which a piece of the world becomes legible to us. A spot where, out of the unsurveyable richness of the whole, a manageable, stable, repeatably measurable quantity emerges.

For such places I use the word interface. An interface is not the observer and not the measuring device. It is, more generally, any physical structure through which a part of the whole becomes something observable in the first place. And the quiet conjecture behind this collection is that the two questions — the one about the whole and the one about the knowable — are connected precisely through this notion. What we can learn about the universe is decided neither at the universe alone nor at us alone, but at the interfaces in between.

It would be tempting to turn this into a grand claim about reality. This collection expressly does not. The complete state of the universe may be taken as real, in its full richness; nothing here disputes that. The only question is which parts of that richness ever become observable quantities for us — and that is not a matter of worldview but one that can be posed and answered precisely in concrete, computable models. The appeal lies exactly in this modesty: a large, blurry question is turned into small, sharp, testable ones.

The two essays in this collection each take up one side of it.

Why interfaces?
The first asks whether the notion of an interface earns its place at all — and shows, in an exactly solvable example, that an interface makes a physical quantity well defined that does not otherwise exist.
Which differences are observable?
The second applies the idea to the beginning of the universe: of two candidate initial states, not every difference survives the path down to our measurements — and one can state a criterion for which differences do and which do not.

Both are deliberately narrow. Neither claims to solve the great riddle. Both do something more modest and more solid: they translate a piece of the large question into a small case one can understand completely. It is in this, and not in a new doctrine of being, that whatever these notes have to offer resides.
