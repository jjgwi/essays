Why Another Perspective?

Quantum theory is arguably the most successful physical theory ever developed. Its predictions have been confirmed with astonishing precision, and it forms the basis of much of modern technology. Nothing in the essays that follow challenges these achievements. The purpose of this website is not to replace quantum mechanics, nor to propose yet another interpretation of it.

Instead, the goal is to ask a rather different question. If every observer is necessarily bounded, as argued in the previous essay, how should quantum physics appear from that viewpoint? This may sound like a small change in perspective, but it leads naturally to questions that are surprisingly difficult to answer.

From States to Observations

Quantum theory is often introduced by starting with the mathematical description of an entire physical system. One defines states, observables and their dynamics, and only afterwards asks what an observer can actually measure. For many purposes this approach is entirely appropriate. It has led to one of the most successful scientific theories ever constructed.

Yet every actual experiment begins at the opposite end. An observer never has access to the complete mathematical description of a physical system. Instead, the observer interacts with only a finite part of it and records only a limited collection of observable quantities. The complete description is therefore not directly available. It must be inferred from observable data.

Observable Physics

This simple observation motivates the viewpoint developed in these essays. Rather than taking the global mathematical description as fundamental, we begin with what is actually observable. The question is no longer simply "What is the quantum state?" but rather "What aspects of the physical world can a bounded observer distinguish?"

This distinction is important. Two mathematical descriptions may differ enormously while producing exactly the same observable physics for a given observer. Conversely, two systems that appear almost identical mathematically may nevertheless differ in ways that remain experimentally accessible. The central task is therefore to understand which differences survive observation and which do not.

A Different Starting Point

Viewed in this way, the problem is not primarily one of quantum interpretation. Questions about wave-function collapse, many worlds or hidden variables are certainly interesting, but they are not the focus here. The concern is more operational. Given only finite observational access, how should physical descriptions be organized? Which mathematical structures naturally represent observable information, and which represent information that lies beyond the observer's reach?

These questions lead naturally toward the framework of algebraic quantum field theory. Unlike formulations that begin with a global wave function, algebraic approaches emphasize collections of observable quantities associated with physical regions. For the viewpoint developed here, this provides a particularly natural language because locality and observability appear from the outset rather than being added later.

Looking Ahead

If observable physics is our starting point, one question immediately follows. How should we describe the relationship between a bounded observer and the larger physical system of which it is only a part? Every observation leaves a measurable imprint, an empirical record of the interaction between observer and observed. Throughout these essays, this observable relationship will be referred to as an interface.

The term is introduced as a conceptual tool rather than as a new physical object. It emphasizes that a bounded observer never has direct access to the complete physical system but only to the information that becomes observable through this interface. The next essay explores this idea in greater detail and asks what properties such interfaces should possess.

Perhaps surprisingly, this discussion eventually leads to the split property in algebraic quantum field theory. Although the split property is often regarded as a rather technical mathematical result, it turns out to capture precisely the kind of separation between observable and inaccessible physics that motivates the present viewpoint. Understanding why this connection exists is one of the central goals of the quantum part of this website.
