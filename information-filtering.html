Filtering, Information Capacity and Spin-2

The previous essay showed that admissible boundary transformations are governed by the static observable structure of the surrounding field. The resulting boundary response determines how observable descriptions adjust under changes of the interface. Surprisingly, this same mechanism has another consequence. It also determines which information can pass through the observable boundary in the first place.

Observable Boundaries as Filters

Every observation is selective. An experimental apparatus never records every degree of freedom present in a physical system. Instead it transmits certain information while suppressing other components. Observable boundaries therefore act as natural filters.

Within the present framework this filtering is not an additional assumption. It follows directly from the same static boundary problem that governs boundary response. The observable boundary does not merely react to changes. It also determines which observable structures remain accessible after those changes. Response and filtering become two aspects of the same underlying mechanism.

Information Capacity

Once observable boundaries are understood as filters, another quantity naturally appears. Every filter possesses a finite capacity. Only a limited amount of distinguishable information can be transmitted through any finite observable interface. This capacity is determined jointly by the observable boundary and the surrounding field configuration.

The environment therefore influences observable physics in two complementary ways. It determines how observable boundaries respond to admissible deformations, and it determines how much information those boundaries can reliably transmit. Both properties arise from the same static observable structure.

A Universal Response

Remarkably, the mathematical description of these filtering properties possesses a highly constrained form. Once consistency, locality and finite information capacity are imposed together, the observable response acquires a universal tensorial structure. The same response appears independently of the detailed microscopic constitution of the underlying field.

This universality is significant. Throughout physics, whenever very different microscopic systems produce the same large-scale behaviour, the common structure usually reflects a deeper organising principle rather than the details of any particular model. The observable boundary appears to exhibit precisely this kind of universality.

Why Spin-2?

The resulting response possesses the characteristic mathematical structure associated with spin-2 fields. Within the present framework this structure is not introduced as a fundamental interaction. Instead it emerges naturally from the observable filtering problem itself. The spin-2 character reflects the geometry of consistent observable response rather than an independent microscopic assumption.

This perspective differs from the traditional route by which spin-2 fields are introduced. Rather than beginning with a field possessing prescribed symmetries, the observable framework begins with finite interfaces, boundary response and information flow. The characteristic spin-2 structure then appears as the unique large-scale organisation compatible with those observable constraints.

Outlook

The central idea developed throughout these essays is remarkably simple. Physical theories are constructed from observations, and every observation takes place through a finite observable boundary. Taking this fact seriously leads naturally to interfaces, observable algebras, boundary response, operational notions of inertia and finally to filtering and information capacity.

Whether this viewpoint ultimately provides a new foundation for quantum theory, gravity or cosmology remains an open question. Its purpose is more modest. It suggests that observable structure is not merely a language for describing physics. It may itself be one of the fundamental ingredients from which physical behaviour emerges.
