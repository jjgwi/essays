Filtering and the Emergence of Gravity

Home

The previous essays argued that observable boundaries do more than separate an observer from the surrounding world. They determine how observable descriptions respond when those boundaries change. A natural question now follows. If observable boundaries control how observations are organised, do they also determine which information can be observed in the first place?

Why Every Observation Filters

Imagine looking at a landscape through a window. The window does not create the landscape, nor does it alter the distant mountains. But it does determine what can be seen. Some information reaches the observer. Other information remains inaccessible. The window acts as a filter.

Every physical observation is similar. No observer has unrestricted access to an entire physical system. Every measurement is made through a finite interface connecting observer and system. Such an interface cannot transmit everything simultaneously. It necessarily selects. Filtering is therefore not a limitation imposed by imperfect instruments. It is an unavoidable consequence of finite observation itself.

This observation extends one of the central themes of these essays. Observable boundaries are not passive surfaces separating two regions of space. They actively determine both how observations are organised and which observable information can pass between observer and system.

Response and Filtering

Within the present framework filtering is not introduced as an additional principle. It emerges from the same static observable structure that governs boundary response. The mathematics that determines how observable descriptions adjust under admissible boundary transformations also determines which observable degrees of freedom remain accessible.

Boundary response and observable filtering therefore describe two aspects of a single underlying structure. One concerns how observable descriptions change. The other concerns what those descriptions are able to contain.

Finite Information Capacity

Once observable boundaries are understood as filters, another question naturally appears. Can any finite interface transmit unlimited information?

The answer appears to be no. Every observable boundary possesses a finite information capacity. Only a limited amount of distinguishable information can pass through any finite interface. This capacity depends not only on the geometry of the boundary itself but also on the surrounding field through which information is transmitted.

The environment therefore influences observable physics in two complementary ways. It determines how observable boundaries respond to admissible changes, and it determines how much information those boundaries can reliably communicate. Both properties arise from the same observable structure.

Towards Gravity

At first sight these conclusions seem to concern only the theory of observation. Surprisingly, they reach much further. Once consistency, locality and finite information capacity are imposed together, the large-scale observable response becomes highly constrained. Many details of the underlying microscopic physics no longer matter. Instead, different microscopic systems begin to exhibit the same effective behaviour.

This kind of universality is familiar throughout physics. Very different microscopic systems can display identical large-scale behaviour when they are governed by the same organising principles. Within the present framework, finite observable boundaries appear to possess exactly this kind of universal response.

The remarkable consequence is that the familiar mathematical structure associated with the gravitational field emerges naturally from these observable constraints. Rather than postulating gravity as the starting point, the observable framework begins with finite observation, boundary response and filtering. The characteristic large-scale gravitational behaviour then appears as the unique organisation compatible with those principles.

Final Outlook

These essays began with a simple observation: every observation has a boundary. Following that idea has led from observable interfaces to observable algebras, from boundary response to operational notions of inertia, and finally to filtering and finite information capacity. Each step has explored another consequence of taking finite observation seriously.

Whether this programme ultimately provides a new foundation for quantum theory, gravity or cosmology remains an open question. Its central proposal is more modest. Observable structure is not merely the language in which physics is described. It may itself be one of the ingredients from which physical behaviour emerges.
