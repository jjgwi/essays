The previous essays introduced observable boundaries as the structures through which physical systems become accessible to observation. They also argued that observable algebras are not merely mathematical conveniences, but the natural language in which bounded observations are organised. The question now changes. If observable boundaries are fundamental ingredients of physical description, do they themselves possess characteristic physical behaviour?

At first this may seem unlikely. A boundary appears to be nothing more than a division between what can and cannot be observed. Yet changing an experimental arrangement always changes that boundary. A detector may be displaced. The accessible region may expand. An apparatus may accelerate. Each of these modifies the observable interface through which the system is described. The natural question is therefore not simply what can be observed, but how the observable structure itself responds.

Static Control

Not every change of an observable boundary is physically equivalent. Some changes exchange energy with the surrounding field through radiation and wave propagation. Others do not. Of particular interest is a special class of boundary transformations that preserve the bookkeeping of the observable description. These transformations alter the observer's local frame while avoiding additional channels of energy transfer. They isolate the response associated with the observable boundary itself.

Remarkably, the response to these transformations is determined without solving the full dynamical evolution of the field. Instead, it is controlled entirely by the static boundary-value problem defined by the field and its surrounding environment. The determining factor is therefore not how disturbances propagate through space and time, but how the observable configuration is already arranged. The response is governed by configuration rather than by radiation.

This distinction is fundamental. Ordinarily we expect acceleration to produce dynamical effects that propagate causally through space. Here, however, the relevant response is established before such propagation becomes important. The observable boundary behaves as though it continually adjusts to the static structure of its environment. The surrounding field determines the response through its configuration rather than through a delayed mechanical interaction.

A Machian Perspective

This viewpoint echoes one of the oldest ideas in theoretical physics. Mach suggested that inertia might not be an intrinsic property of isolated matter but instead reflect its relationship with the rest of the universe. For more than a century physicists have searched for a precise realization of this intuition.

The present viewpoint arrives at a related conclusion by a different route. The environment indeed influences inertial behaviour, but not because distant matter continually exerts forces upon an accelerating object. Instead, the influence appears through the static observable configuration established by the surrounding field. In this sense the environment enters as a property of observable structure rather than as a direct dynamical interaction.

Boundary Response

Once the static observable configuration has been identified, changing the observable boundary requires the corresponding observable description to adjust consistently. This adjustment is what we call the boundary response.

Boundary response is not an additional force and it is not a material surface. Rather, it is the characteristic change required to preserve a consistent observable description under admissible deformations of the boundary. Because the response is determined by the surrounding static configuration, it reflects both the physical field and the observable environment through which that field is accessed.

The important point is that the response is determined independently of any particular definition of inertial mass. The observable structure responds first. Operational notions of inertia emerge only afterwards as different ways of measuring that response.

Towards Inertia

This observation leads to an unexpected possibility. Physics usually speaks of the inertial mass of an object, suggesting that inertia is characterised by a single quantity. From the perspective of boundary response, however, different experiments probe different aspects of the observable response. There is therefore no fundamental reason why every operational definition of inertia should coincide.

The next essay develops this idea by introducing two distinct notions of inertial response. One is associated with accelerating an observable boundary. The other is associated with its reactive response. Although these quantities are operationally different, ordinary mechanics almost always observes only one inertial mass. Understanding why this happens becomes one of the central questions of the theory.

The answer turns out to be remarkably simple. In empty space the two operational responses become locked together by the common boundary response determined by the vacuum itself. The uniqueness of inertial mass is therefore not assumed at the outset. It emerges as a special property of the observable vacuum.

Once material environments are introduced, this locking need no longer remain exact. The observable response becomes increasingly sensitive to the structure of the surrounding field. This naturally leads to questions of filtering, information capacity and, ultimately, to the appearance of spin-2 behaviour that will be explored in the following essays.
