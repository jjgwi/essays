The Cosmological Setting

The finch story has something cosmology does not: comparison by repetition. The Grants could observe a population before a drought, measure it again afterwards, and compare. Nature performed the experiment; the biologists recorded the change.

Cosmology gives us no such repeated experiment. We observe one universe, one cosmic history, one long development from the early universe to the world we now inhabit. There is no second universe next door, no rerun of the Big Bang, and no way to watch cosmic history unfold again under slightly different conditions.

Yet quantum cosmology does not naturally begin with one finished classical history. It begins with a quantum state of the universe. Such a state may contain many possible histories at once. These possible histories are often called branches.

Basic idea: quantum cosmology describes a universe whose quantum state may support many possible classical-looking histories. The question is how probabilities should be assigned to those histories.

Branches

A branch is a possible history of the universe that has become stable enough to be treated as a classical alternative. One branch might describe a universe with one amount of inflation, another with a different amount. One might contain many galaxies and observers; another might contain fewer. One might contain data like ours many times; another might contain them rarely or not at all.

A branch is not just a label invented after the fact. It is supposed to correspond to an alternative that the physical theory itself can support and distinguish. In this essay, it is enough to think of branches as possible classical-looking cosmic histories.

The point is not that we can observe all branches. We cannot. The point is that the theory may assign probabilities by comparing the branch we observe with the other branches the same quantum state allows.

Decoherence

If the universe is quantum, why does it look classical? Decoherence is part of the answer. It is the process by which different alternatives stop interfering with one another. Once that happens, each alternative can behave like a separate classical world, with its own records and internal history.

It is tempting to say that decoherence selects one branch. But that is too quick. Decoherence mainly selects a branch structure: a set of alternatives that can be treated as classical for the purposes of assigning probabilities. Whether one branch is actually selected, whether all branches persist, or how to interpret the probabilities is a deeper interpretive question.

Decoherence does not by itself answer which branch we should expect to observe. It makes the question meaningful by identifying the branches among which probabilities can be assigned.

Why Other Branches Enter Probability

A natural worry arises immediately. If we observe one classical world, why should other branches affect its probability?

The answer is that the other branches do not causally affect our branch. They do not push on it or change what happens inside it. They enter because probabilities are comparative.

If a rule assigns a weight to each branch, the probability of one branch is its weight compared with the total weight of all relevant branches. The other branches affect the comparison class, not the local physics inside our branch.

The probability of one branch depends not only on its own weight, but on the weights of the other branches it is being compared with.

This is not special to cosmology. If ten people are in a room and two are doctors, the probability that a randomly chosen person is a doctor depends on the whole room. If more non-doctors enter, the doctors have not changed, but the probability has. The comparison group changed.

Conditioning in quantum cosmology works in the same spirit. It does not say that unrealized branches physically alter our world. It says that our probability assignment is obtained by comparing all relevant branches under a specified rule.

Conditioning Proposals

A quantum state plus a branch structure does not automatically give the final probabilities for observations. One still needs a rule telling us how the branches should count. Such a rule is called here a conditioning proposal.

Different conditioning proposals can begin with the same branches but produce different probabilities. The histories remain the same; the translation into predictions changes.

Object Plain meaning
Branch family The possible classical-looking histories supplied by the theory
Bottom-up weight The original quantum weight of a branch before observational conditioning
Conditioning proposal A rule that reweights branches when making predictions
Prediction The final probability distribution over branches or observations

Bottom-Up and Top-Down Cosmology

A bottom-up question begins with the initial quantum state and the laws of physics, then asks what histories follow. This is the most familiar direction of explanation: start at the beginning and evolve forward.

A top-down question begins with some present data and asks how probabilities should be assigned to histories compatible with those data. In cosmology this matters because a large universe may contain many possible locations where data like ours occur.

The contrast can be stated simply:

Viewpoint Question
Bottom-up What histories does the quantum state produce?
Top-down Given that data like ours exist, how should histories be weighted?

Volume Weighting

One influential conditioning idea is volume weighting. Some cosmic histories produce more spatial volume than others. A larger universe may contain more galaxies, more planets, more observers, or more possible locations where our data could occur. Volume weighting says that such histories should count more.

In inflationary cosmology, volume can grow extremely rapidly as inflation lasts longer. A small difference in the duration of inflation can therefore produce a huge difference in the amount of space produced.

Volume weighting is simple and powerful, but it can also lead to problems. If total volume is rewarded too strongly, very large histories can dominate the probability assignment, even when they are observationally troubling.

Volume Averaging

Volume averaging responds differently. Instead of rewarding a branch simply because it contains more total volume, it asks for something closer to an average or density of observations per unit volume.

In a simple homogeneous model, volume averaging may behave much like the original bottom-up weighting. More generally, it can depend on how dense ordinary observations or observer-like data are in each branch.

The important contrast is:

Prescription What it emphasizes
Volume weighting Total volume or total number of observational opportunities
Volume averaging Per-volume density or average observational weight

Effective Replication Opportunities

A branch has a basic probability. That is its starting weight in the theory. But this is not the whole story. A branch may also contain many places where something like our situation could arise.

Imagine two possible cosmic histories. In the first, there is only one place where observers like us might appear. In the second, there are many such places. The second branch does not necessarily have a larger basic probability. It may even have a smaller one. But it gives more chances for data like ours to occur somewhere inside it.

This is what we mean by effective replication opportunities. The term does not mean that there are perfect copies of us. It means that a branch may contain more or fewer chances for the relevant kind of observation to happen.

A branch probability tells us how strongly the theory weights that history. Effective replication opportunities tell us how many chances that history gives for data like ours to appear.

These are different ideas. A branch can have a high basic probability but few opportunities for observers like us. Another branch can have a low basic probability but many opportunities. Once observations are included, these two features may pull in different directions.

The Hartle-Hertog Occurrence Idea

Hartle and Hertog introduced a simple but important way to think about this. Instead of asking only how probable a branch is, or only how large it is, ask another question:

What is the chance that data like ours occur somewhere in this branch?

This changes the emphasis. A branch is not rewarded simply because it is large. It is rewarded because its size, structure, and contents make it more or less likely that the relevant data occur somewhere inside it.

A small branch may give the data only a few chances to appear. A larger branch may give many more chances. At first, more chances make a big difference. But after a certain point, adding still more chances matters less, because the data were already very likely to occur somewhere.

A simple analogy is rolling dice. If you roll once, getting a six is uncertain. If you roll many times, the chance of seeing at least one six becomes much larger. But after many rolls, adding one more roll changes little: you were already very likely to have seen a six somewhere.

Hartle-Hertog top-down weighting applies this kind of reasoning to cosmic branches. It asks whether a branch contains at least one occurrence of the data being conditioned on.

The Hartle-Hertog idea is not simply “bigger branches count more.” It is: branches count according to the chance that data like ours occur somewhere inside them.

This is why the idea is called top-down. We begin from the fact that observations like ours exist, and then ask how that fact changes the probabilities assigned to the possible histories.
