A Small Difference Becomes Decisive

In 1977 a severe drought struck Daphne Major. The shortage of rain dramatically reduced the availability of small, soft seeds, leaving mostly larger and harder ones behind. Almost overnight the environment confronted the finches with a new challenge. Birds that had previously lived under nearly identical conditions now faced a very different ecological landscape.

The remarkable point is that the drought did not create these differences. The birds already differed slightly before the environmental change. For many years those variations had little practical consequence because food was abundant and many feeding strategies were successful. Once the drought altered the available food supply, however, one particular difference suddenly became decisive. A slightly deeper beak now meant a greater chance of survival.

This was precisely the kind of mechanism Darwin had imagined more than a century earlier. The environment did not redesign the birds. It changed the conditions under which they lived, and those new conditions determined which existing differences became biologically important. Natural selection therefore acted not by creating variation, but by changing its significance.

Equally important is what happened afterwards. The surviving population became more homogeneous with respect to beak depth. Yet evolution did not stop. Future environmental changes may favour entirely different traits. A characteristic that is almost irrelevant today may become the defining difference tomorrow. Every observed population is therefore both the endpoint of countless earlier episodes of evolution and the starting point for countless future ones. The environment continually changes which differences matter.

This observation reaches far beyond biology. It suggests a more general question. Can external conditions determine which differences become physically important without creating those differences themselves? If so, the same underlying idea might appear in other areas of science. The remainder of this essay asks whether something structurally similar could also occur in cosmology.
