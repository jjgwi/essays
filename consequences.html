Tiny Differences, Big Consequences

The previous chapter asked whether a proposed conditioning can consistently be regarded as part of the physical theory. Suppose that it can. What consequences would follow?

The answer is surprisingly general. A physical mechanism cannot preserve every possible distinction. Like every physical process, it possesses only a finite capacity to maintain meaningful differences while allowing others to fade away. The role of conditioning is therefore not to create new histories, but to determine which distinctions between existing branch histories remain physically significant.

This idea appears in remarkably different areas of science. Although the mechanisms themselves are unrelated, they share a common structure: an external constraint determines which distinctions continue to matter and which gradually disappear.

Example External constraint Distinctions that cannot be maintained
Darwin's finches The ecological environment after the drought Small differences in beak shape that no longer influence survival
Finite-radius gravity (DtN) The enclosing boundary and its elliptic response Arbitrarily detailed information about the surrounding environment
Cosmological conditioning A physical conditioning mechanism Arbitrarily fine distinctions between branch histories

Darwin's finches illustrate this principle particularly well. The population observed before the drought was itself the outcome of countless earlier episodes of evolution. The drought did not create differences between the birds. It acted upon differences that already existed, preserving some while allowing others to disappear. The surviving population then became the starting point for future evolution, where new distinctions may eventually become important.

Cosmology differs because we have access to only one realized history. We cannot observe earlier branch histories or watch a conditioning mechanism acting upon them. The mathematical framework therefore asks a different question: if such a mechanism exists, what kinds of distinctions could it consistently preserve?

The forest provides an everyday analogy. Without asking a specific question, the two landscapes appear almost indistinguishable. Once attention is directed towards a single feature, however, a difference that previously seemed insignificant suddenly becomes decisive. The filter has not created the distinction. It has simply determined which aspect of the comparison matters.

The mathematical work described in these essays explores whether an analogous principle may exist in quantum cosmology. Not by creating new histories, but by determining which distinctions among existing branch histories can remain physically significant. Whether nature actually performs such a conditioning remains an open scientific question. The consistency audit identifies those proposals that are compatible with the known structure of quantum cosmology and therefore deserve to be considered as genuine candidates for physics.

Darwin's finches taught us that evolution is not driven by creating differences, but by acting upon differences that already exist. These essays ask whether modern cosmology may contain a surprisingly similar idea: that the laws of physics themselves determine which distinctions survive, which disappear, and ultimately which history becomes the one we observe. For now, that remains an open question—but perhaps a worthwhile one to ask.
