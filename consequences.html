What the Framework Establishes

The previous essay introduced the cosmological setting. Quantum cosmology may supply many possible branch histories. Conditioning proposals tell us how those branches should be weighted when making predictions. Volume weighting, volume averaging, and top-down replication are different ways of doing this.

This essay asks the next question:

Once different conditioning proposals are written down, how do we tell whether their differences actually matter?

The framework does not choose the correct proposal. It does not solve the cosmological measure problem. Its contribution is more specific: it provides a way to compare proposals by the probability distributions they induce over branches and observable branch properties.

Prediction Space

Suppose a model gives three possible branches. A probability assignment over those branches might be:

(0.90, 0.09, 0.01)

This means that the first branch receives most of the probability, the second receives some, and the third receives very little.

Another proposal might assign:

(0.00, 0.00, 1.00)

which means the third branch dominates.

The set of all such probability assignments is the space of predictions. For three branches, this space can be pictured as a triangle. Each corner is a prediction where one branch receives all the probability. Points inside the triangle spread probability among several branches.

The framework compares conditioning proposals by asking where they land in this prediction space.

Reach

Reach measures how much of a formal difference between proposals survives as a difference in predictions.

If two proposals are written differently but produce the same branch distribution, then their Reach is small, or even zero, for that branch family. They may differ as formulas, but the difference is invisible in the resulting predictions.

If two proposals move probability to different branches or different observable properties, their Reach is large. Then the formal difference has survived into prediction.

Reach asks not whether two proposals are different on paper, but whether they make different predictions.
Question Reach reformulation
Are the formulas different? Do they produce different branch distributions?
Which proposal is more natural? Where does each proposal land in prediction space?
Can observers tell them apart? Does the difference survive in observable branch properties?

The Audit Procedure

The framework can be summarized as an audit procedure.

Step Question
1. Branches What physically meaningful branch histories are being compared?
2. Prescriptions How does each conditioning proposal weight those branches?
3. Distributions What probability distribution does each proposal produce?
4. Distance How far apart are those distributions?
5. Observables Does the difference remain visible after coarse-graining to observable properties?

This procedure is deliberately modest. It does not decide which proposal is true. It decides whether two proposals make distinguishable predictions on a specified branch family.

Three Ways Differences Can Disappear or Survive

The mathematical notes describe three mechanisms that shape prediction space. In this essay they can be stated plainly.

1. Quotienting

Some parts of a proposal may be invisible on a given branch family. If two formulas assign the same weights to the branches being compared, they produce the same prediction, even if they differ elsewhere.

2. Capacity

Some response rules may not be able to support many alternatives at once. In prediction space, this means they can reach only lower-dimensional regions, such as edges or corners rather than the full interior.

3. Concentration

Some families of prescriptions formally have many possibilities but still concentrate near one branch. Volume weighting is an example: if one branch has much larger volume than the others, steep volume weighting can push the prediction close to that branch's corner.

These mechanisms are background tools. The main application below concerns the third kind of effect: how volume weighting and top-down replication move probability through prediction space.

The Live Application

The live application is the dispute between volume weighting, volume averaging, and Hartle-Hertog top-down replication.

The previous essay explained the vocabulary. Here we ask what the framework establishes about the comparison.

There are two familiar limiting regimes.

Regime Top-down behaviour
Data rare in every branch Top-down weighting is close to volume weighting
Data common in every branch Top-down weighting is close to bottom-up or volume-averaged weighting

These two statements are not the new point. They are the expected limiting behaviours. The new point concerns the mixed regime.

The Mixed Regime

The mixed regime occurs when data like ours are rare in some branches but already saturated in others. That is, some branches provide too few opportunities for the data to appear easily, while others provide so many opportunities that occurrence is almost guaranteed.

It is natural to picture top-down weighting as a compromise between two endpoints: volume weighting on one side and bottom-up or volume-averaged weighting on the other.

The framework shows that this picture can be wrong.

In the mixed regime, top-down replication can become a third prediction, far from both volume weighting and the bottom-up or volume-averaged baseline.

The reason is that top-down weighting favours branches near the crossover: branches with enough replication opportunities to matter, but not so many that the occurrence factor has completely saturated.

A Three-Branch Example

Consider three branches.

Branch Prior weight Replication opportunities Favoured by
Branch 1 Large Small Bottom-up weighting
Branch 2 Medium Large but not overwhelming Top-down crossover
Branch 3 Small Huge Volume weighting

Bottom-up weighting prefers Branch 1, because it begins with the largest prior probability. Volume weighting prefers Branch 3, because its replication count is enormous. But top-down replication can prefer Branch 2, because that branch lies near the transition between rarity and saturation.

A concrete numerical example uses prior weights:

(0.90, 0.09, 0.01)

and replication opportunities:

(1, 104, 108)

In this example, the bottom-up rule mostly selects the first branch, volume weighting mostly selects the third, and the regulated top-down rule can put most of its weight on the second.

The branch favoured by top-down replication need not be favoured by either endpoint prescription.

What the Result Means

The result does not show that top-down replication is correct. It does not show that volume weighting or volume averaging is wrong. It shows something narrower and more diagnostic.

The top-down prescription is not merely a compromise between two known poles. It can occupy a different region of prediction space. In suitable branch families, it can be far from both bottom-up weighting and volume weighting at the same time.

That means the dispute is not one-dimensional. It is not enough to ask whether we should move toward or away from volume weighting. We must also ask whether replication regulation identifies branches that neither endpoint favours.

What to Check in a Real Cosmological Model

The framework identifies the structure to look for.

Look for branch families where prior weight decreases while replication opportunities increase.

In such a family, bottom-up weighting favours the high-prior branches, volume weighting favours the high-replication branches, and top-down replication may favour intermediate crossover branches.

Whether realistic inflationary cosmology contains such branch families is a question for detailed model building. The framework does not answer it by itself. It says what comparison must be made.

What the Framework Does Not Establish

The framework does not solve the cosmological measure problem. It does not select the correct conditioning proposal. It does not eliminate the need for assumptions about observers, data, self-location, or xerographic distributions.

It also does not explain why decoherence produces the particular experienced world we find ourselves in. Decoherence supplies a branch structure. The framework compares probability assignments on that structure.

So the framework is not a complete theory of quantum cosmology. It is an audit tool.

What the Framework Does Establish

The framework establishes a sharper way to compare proposals.

It does not ask only... It asks instead...
Are the formulas different? Do they produce different branch distributions?
Which proposal sounds more natural? Where does each proposal land in prediction space?
Does top-down weighting have the right limiting behaviour? What happens in the mixed regime between the limits?
Does the framework solve the measure problem? No. It provides a diagnostic for comparing candidate measures.

The central lesson is:

A conditioning proposal matters scientifically only insofar as it changes the distribution of observable branch predictions.

Some formal differences disappear. Some survive. Some appear only in special regimes. The purpose of the framework is to distinguish these cases.

Returning to the Finches

Darwin did not discover that finches differ. Anyone could see that. The scientific achievement was to understand which differences mattered under which environmental pressures.

The cosmological case is far more abstract, but the logic is similar. We already have different conditioning proposals. We already have possible branch histories. The question is which differences between proposals survive into predictions about what observers can see.

That is what Reach is designed to measure.

The important step is from “these formulas differ” to “these formulas make different observable predictions.”

That is what the framework establishes.
