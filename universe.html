A Universe Without a Before

The story of Darwin's finches is convincing because it possesses something that many areas of science take for granted. There is a before. The original population was measured before the drought, the environment changed, and the surviving population was measured afterwards. The entire process could therefore be reconstructed directly from observation.

Cosmology therefore places us in a fundamentally different situation. Unlike the Grants, we cannot compare the universe before and after some cosmic selection has taken place. There is no earlier population available for observation and no direct record of how our own cosmic history came to be realized. We observe only one universe, already containing the outcome of its entire evolution.

To understand the problem, it helps to clarify what cosmologists mean by different histories. They do not mean many independent universes existing side by side. Rather, quantum cosmology describes a branching structure containing many physically admissible ways in which a universe like ours could evolve. Our observations correspond to only one realized branch of that structure.

The existence of these branches is part of the quantum description itself. The scientific question is therefore not whether alternative histories can be imagined. It is whether the laws of physics contain a mechanism capable of distinguishing among them in a physically meaningful way.

Darwin's finches Quantum cosmology
A population of real birds A branching structure of physically admissible histories
Each bird represents one individual Each branch represents one possible cosmic history
The population can be observed before selection The branching structure cannot be observed directly
Natural selection acts on the population A proposed conditioning acts on the branching structure
A new population emerges and can be measured One realized history is observed

This difference is not merely practical. It changes the logic of the scientific question. In biology we can reconstruct the mechanism because both the initial and the final populations are available for observation. In cosmology the branching structure itself remains inaccessible. Only one realized history is ever observed.

Nevertheless, many approaches to quantum cosmology introduce conditioning. Some histories contribute more strongly to the final predictions than others, and this can dramatically change what different theories appear to predict. Mathematically this resembles the role played by environmental selection in biology. Whether it also corresponds to a genuine physical process is a much deeper question.

This is precisely where the analogy with Darwin reaches its limit. In biology there is little doubt about what performs the selection. The changing environment is directly observed, and its influence on the population can be reconstructed from evidence. In cosmology we do not observe any analogous mechanism acting on the branching structure. Its existence must therefore be justified in a different way.

The mathematical work discussed in these essays approaches this problem by asking a different question. Rather than beginning with a proposed conditioning and assuming that it is physical, it asks whether such a conditioning can consistently belong to the theory itself. The next chapter develops a mathematical framework for examining precisely that question.
