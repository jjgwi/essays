A Universe Without a Before

The story of Darwin's finches is compelling because it contains something that cosmology does not. Peter and Rosemary Grant observed a real population before the drought, measured individual birds, and returned afterwards to discover which birds had survived. Nature itself performed the experiment.

Cosmology therefore faces a fundamentally different problem. We observe only one realized history of the universe. There is no second universe against which our own can be compared, and no way to repeat cosmic evolution under slightly different conditions. If different possible histories are to be compared at all, the comparison must take place within the mathematical framework of the theory itself.

Quantum cosmology therefore considers many admissible branch histories. Each branch represents one possible evolution compatible with the underlying quantum description. Our observations correspond to only one realized branch, but the theory contains many neighbouring alternatives that differ only slightly from one another.

Learning from the Grants

The Grants were interested in much more than identifying which birds survived. They wanted to understand why. Different scientific explanations of the drought were possible. Perhaps only the very hardest seeds remained available. Perhaps the drought was slightly less severe and softer seeds also survived. A scientist therefore compares not only observations, but also competing explanations.

Imagine two ecological models describing the same drought. Model A assumes that the drought was extremely severe. Only birds with the deepest beaks can crack the remaining seeds. Model B assumes a slightly milder drought. A somewhat wider range of birds can now survive. The important point is that both models act on exactly the same population of finches.

The interesting scientific question is no longer simply which birds survive. It is how differently the two models shape the same population. If they classify almost every bird in the same way, then the distinction between the two ecological explanations has little practical consequence. If they classify neighbouring birds differently, then even a very small change in the description of the drought produces a measurable change in the population that emerges afterwards.

From Comparison to Reach

The example of the two drought models illustrates a general scientific problem. The two explanations begin by making different assumptions about the environment. Nature then acts on exactly the same population of birds. What finally reaches the researcher is not the drought itself, but the surviving population. The important question is therefore: How much of the original difference between the two ecological models survives after both have acted upon the same population?

If the two models ultimately shape the population in almost exactly the same way, then most of their original distinction has been lost. If, on the other hand, they produce noticeably different patterns of survival, then the distinction between the two explanations has largely been preserved. The comparison is therefore no longer between two droughts. It is between the effects that those drought models produce on the same population.

This is precisely the intuition behind the mathematical notion of Reach. Reach does not simply ask whether two scientific descriptions are different. It asks how much of their original distinction survives after both have acted upon the same underlying physical system. In biology, that system is the population of finches. In quantum cosmology, it is the branching structure of possible histories.

Both act on the same finch population. Both act on the same branching structure.
Each model shapes the population differently. Each conditioning shapes the branching structure differently.
The comparison asks how much of the original distinction survives after natural selection. The comparison asks how much of the original distinction survives in branch-realized predictions.

This is precisely the kind of comparison that motivates the mathematical work described in the accompanying notes. The goal is not merely to decide whether two conditioning proposals are different. The deeper question is how much of their original distinction survives after both have acted on the same branching structure. Developing a quantitative language for making this comparison is one of the central objectives of the framework.
