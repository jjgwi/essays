A Universe Without a Before

The story of Darwin's finches is convincing because it possesses something that many areas of science take for granted. There is a before. The original population was measured before the drought, the environment changed, and the surviving population was measured afterwards. The entire sequence could be reconstructed from direct observation.

Cosmology therefore places us in a fundamentally different situation. Unlike the Grants, we cannot compare the universe before and after some cosmic selection has taken place. There is no earlier population of universes waiting to be measured, no observational record against which our own universe can be compared. We observe only one universe, already containing the outcome of its entire history.

To understand the problem, it helps to clarify what cosmologists mean by possible histories. They do not mean many universes existing side by side. Rather, they mean the different mathematically possible ways in which a universe like ours could have evolved while remaining compatible with the known laws of physics. Quantum cosmology studies this entire landscape of possibilities, even though only one history is ultimately observed. The central question is whether some principle of conditioning can explain why our observed history emerges from among those possibilities.

Darwin's finches Quantum cosmology
A population of real birds A mathematical ensemble of possible histories
Each bird represents one individual Each history represents one possible evolution of our universe
The population can be observed before selection The ensemble cannot be observed before conditioning
Natural selection acts on the population A conditioning prescription acts on the ensemble
A new population emerges and can be measured One realized history is observed

This difference is not merely practical. It changes the logic of the scientific question. In biology we can ask whether natural selection truly produced the observed shift because both the initial and the final populations are available. In cosmology, by contrast, the ensemble of mathematically possible histories is not something we can observe directly. There is nothing resembling the pre-drought measurements that made the work of Peter and Rosemary Grant so compelling.

Nevertheless, cosmologists often compare different theories by introducing conditioning rules. These assign greater importance to some possible histories than to others, and the resulting predictions may differ dramatically. Differences that originally seemed almost negligible can become the dominant distinction once such a conditioning has been imposed. Mathematically, this resembles the filtering performed by the drought, although the physical interpretation remains much less clear.

There is, however, one crucial difference. In the biological case we know that nature itself performed the filtering. The drought was an independently observed physical event. In cosmology this is precisely the point at issue. Are these conditioning rules merely tools that help us analyse the landscape of possible histories? Or do they correspond to something that the universe itself does?

The strategy that worked so well for Darwin's finches cannot answer this question. Without an observable population preceding the conditioning, there is no direct empirical anchor against which the filtering can be tested. Instead, the question must be reformulated. Can a proposed conditioning rule be consistently understood as part of the physical theory itself?

This is the problem addressed by the mathematical work on which the remainder of this essay is based. Rather than assuming that every useful conditioning procedure reflects a law of nature, it asks which conditioning rules can genuinely be realised within the structure of quantum cosmology.
