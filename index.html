Notes on Physics

Plain-language essays on what a finite observer can control, measure, and learn from the surrounding physical world.

The essays collect interpretational lessons from technical models. The aim is not to replace the mathematics, but to explain what the results appear to say—and to keep a clear line between what has been demonstrated in a model and what remains a proposal for future work.

Notes on quantum interfaces and observation

How parts of a quantum world become separately usable, how records are formed, and why some differences in a global wavefunction may never appear in the observations available to us.

  • What this is about
    An introduction to the physical structures that turn parts of a quantum world into controllable systems, lasting records, and observable facts.
  • Why interfaces?
    Why distance and causal separation are not always enough to make two regions independent in quantum field theory—and how a finite buffer can supply the missing structure.
  • Which differences are observable?
    Why two proposed quantum states of the universe can be mathematically different yet observationally identical, and how physical records decide which differences survive.

Notes on inertia and boundary response

How a finite boundary turns the surrounding world into a measurable response, why there are different operational meanings of inertia, and how much environmental information those measurements can retain.

  • What this is about
    A nontechnical historical introduction, from Newton’s absolute space through Mach, Einstein, and Sciama to a finite-boundary question about inertia and the surrounding world.
  • How can a boundary define inertia?
    A plain-language guide to the Dirichlet-to-Neumann response map, reactive and translational inertia, and the model result that locks them together in vacuum but lets matter separate them.
  • How much of the environment can inertia remember?
    Why a finite boundary acts as a distance-dependent information filter, what “finite capacity” means at realistic precision, and what would still be required to extend the result from a scalar model to gravity.
