Seeing the World

Physics begins with observation. Every scientific theory is ultimately judged by comparing its predictions with experiments or observations. We point telescopes toward the sky, build particle accelerators, lower detectors into deep mines and send spacecraft to distant planets. Through these instruments we gradually assemble an increasingly detailed picture of nature.

For centuries this picture appeared remarkably straightforward. The universe exists independently of us, and science attempts to describe it as accurately as possible. Better instruments reveal more details, more accurate measurements reduce uncertainty, and more powerful theories explain a wider range of phenomena. Scientific progress therefore seems to consist of steadily removing the limitations of observation.

This view has been extraordinarily successful. Newton's mechanics described falling apples and planetary motion using the same mathematical laws. Maxwell unified electricity, magnetism and light. Modern particle physics explains an astonishing range of experimental results using a comparatively small number of fundamental principles.

Throughout these developments the observer occupied a surprisingly modest role. Observers performed experiments, but they did not appear in the laws themselves. The equations did not depend on who carried out a measurement or where it was performed. Observation was regarded as a practical activity rather than a fundamental ingredient of physical theory.

Hidden beneath this success, however, was an assumption that was so natural it was rarely noticed. The observer was implicitly treated as someone who could, at least in principle, obtain access to the complete physical description of the world. Real scientists obviously never achieve this, but their limitations were usually regarded as practical rather than conceptual. Better instruments would simply reveal a larger portion of an already existing reality.

Information Has a History

During the nineteenth century this picture began to change. Light travels with a finite speed, so information does not arrive instantaneously. Astronomers never see distant galaxies as they are today but as they were millions or even billions of years ago. Looking deeper into space is therefore simultaneously looking further into the past.

Observation is not merely a passive act of looking. It is a physical process that depends on how information propagates through the universe. What we know depends not only on what exists but also on where we are, when we observe and how information reaches us.

The Observer Becomes Local

Einstein transformed this realization into one of the central principles of modern physics. Because no signal travels faster than light, every observer has access only to a limited portion of spacetime. Two observers located in different places literally possess different information because different physical signals reach them.

This represents a profound conceptual shift. The observer has acquired a location. Observation has become local. Nevertheless, relativity still assumes one underlying spacetime shared by all observers. Different observers possess different information, but they remain observers of the same physical universe.

Quantum Theory Changes the Question

Quantum mechanics pushes the role of observation even further. Measurements, observables and experiments now lie at the heart of the theory. Much discussion has focused on what measurements do and how they should be interpreted. The approach taken on this website begins with a different question. Instead of asking what measurements do, it asks what can actually be measured.

No laboratory interacts with an entire quantum system. Every detector responds only to certain physical quantities, and every experiment samples only a tiny fraction of all possible information. The important question therefore becomes which aspects of a physical system are actually accessible to a finite observer.

Cosmology Makes the Problem Unavoidable

Cosmology presents perhaps the clearest illustration of this situation. There is only one observable universe and only one cosmic history available to us. Everything we know about the early universe is reconstructed from signals that happen to reach us today. Our knowledge is therefore necessarily incomplete, not simply because our telescopes are imperfect or because our theories are unfinished, but because every observation begins at one particular place inside the universe. No observer ever stands outside the cosmos to view it as a whole.

A Common Historical Trend

Classical mechanics, relativity, quantum theory and cosmology are usually taught as separate subjects, each with its own mathematical language. Seen from a broader perspective, however, they reveal a common historical development. Again and again physics has been forced to recognize that observers are finite. Information travels at finite speed, measurements are local and every experiment has access to only a limited part of the physical world. What once appeared to be merely practical limitations have gradually become part of the conceptual foundations of physics.

Beginning with the Observer

This historical development motivates the viewpoint adopted throughout this website. Instead of beginning with the complete universe and asking how it should be described, we begin with the observer and ask a different question:

What constitutes observable physics?

A bounded observer is not simply someone equipped with imperfect instruments. Rather, the observer occupies a finite region of space, interacts locally with the surrounding world and receives only finite information from beyond that region. The boundary of this region is therefore more than a geometric surface. It marks the limit of direct observational access. Across that boundary information may enter, information may leave and information may remain forever inaccessible.

Two Research Programmes

Once observation becomes the starting point, familiar problems appear in a different light. The first part of this website investigates how observable descriptions arise in quantum theory, why interfaces become necessary and how the split property provides one important realization of these ideas within algebraic quantum field theory. The second part asks whether boundaries may also play an active role in gravitation by studying how finite regions respond to their surroundings. Although these research programmes employ different mathematical frameworks, they are united by a common methodological principle: begin with the finite observer rather than with the complete universe.

Looking Ahead

The essays that follow do not propose a new interpretation of quantum mechanics or gravitation. Instead, they explore a simple but far-reaching idea. If every physical observation is necessarily bounded, perhaps our formulation of physics should begin there.

Every observation begins somewhere. Every experiment has a location. Every measurement has a horizon. Every observer has a boundary.
