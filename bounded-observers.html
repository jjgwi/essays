Physics seeks to understand the universe.

At first sight this seems straightforward. We observe nature, develop mathematical theories and compare their predictions with experiment. Better instruments reveal more detail, better theories explain more phenomena, and our understanding gradually improves.

Yet modern physics has taught us that the situation is more subtle. Every observation begins somewhere. Every experiment is performed from one particular place in the universe. Every observer has access to only a limited part of reality. Our knowledge is therefore shaped not only by the universe itself, but also by the physical conditions under which observation becomes possible.

At the same time, the universe presents a complementary challenge. Not every difference can remain physically meaningful. Environmental constraints, boundaries and the laws of physics determine which distinctions nature is capable of preserving and which inevitably disappear.

Most dinosaur footprints disappeared within days. Only under exceptional conditions did a tiny fraction become fossilized and survive for millions of years. Nature does not preserve every event equally. The history we are able to reconstruct depends both on the information that reaches us and on the traces that the physical world has managed to retain.

Understanding therefore requires two things: information must reach us, and nature must preserve something worth observing.

These two questions motivate the work collected on this website.

What can a limited observer know about the universe?

What distinctions can the universe itself maintain?

Although these questions arise in different areas of physics, they are deeply connected. Observation depends on the information that reaches us; prediction depends on the distinctions that nature itself is capable of sustaining.

The first research programme explores how observable descriptions emerge in quantum physics and why every observation is necessarily local. The second investigates how physical constraints shape the responses available to gravitating systems and, ultimately, how they may determine which distinctions remain physically meaningful in cosmology.

Together, these essays explore two complementary paths toward the same goal: understanding how we come to know the universe, and how the universe itself determines what can be known.
