Every Observation Has a Boundary

Home

Every observation begins from somewhere. A laboratory occupies a finite region, a telescope sees only a limited part of the sky, and every detector responds only to particular physical interactions. Physics therefore always distinguishes between the world itself and the information available to an observer.

This distinction is often hidden because it causes no practical difficulty in everyday experiments. Yet it becomes central in modern physics. Cosmology asks what can be learned about the whole universe from one location. Quantum theory asks which properties remain observable when only certain measurements are available. Gravitation asks how a finite region responds to the surrounding universe.

These essays adopt the viewpoint that a bounded observer is not a limitation to be ignored but a physical fact to be understood.

The common question is simple: what information survives the journey from the world to a finite observer?

The following essays examine two answers to that question. One uses algebraic ideas from quantum theory. The other studies physical boundary response in gravitation.
