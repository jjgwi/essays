How can a boundary define inertia?

From a surface response map to two different ways of measuring resistance to motion

We usually speak of inertia as if it were a simple property carried by an object: the more mass it has, the more strongly it resists acceleration. That description works extremely well in ordinary mechanics. But it leaves a deeper question untouched. Before asking what numerical value inertia has, we can ask what physical arrangement makes a local inertial response measurable in the first place.

The models discussed here begin with a deliberately finite observer: a laboratory enclosed by an imagined surface. The surface need not be a material wall. It marks the division between the system we control and the exterior that we do not control directly. The central claim is modest but unusual:

A local inertial response is not described as a property of the body alone. It is a response of a body-and-boundary arrangement to the world outside that boundary.

The boundary as a question-and-answer device

Suppose a field fills the exterior of a spherical laboratory. We can prescribe a pattern of field values on the spherical surface and ask how the exterior must respond. In particular, we can ask how steeply the field points outward at each point of the surface.

The mathematical object that answers this question is called a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, usually shortened to DtN map. The name is less important than the idea:

  • we specify the field pattern on the boundary;
  • the exterior equations determine the field outside;
  • the map returns the outward slope, flux, or stress at the boundary.

It is rather like tapping the wall of a hidden room and learning about the room from the way the wall pushes back. The map does not hand us a complete picture of everything outside. It compresses the exterior into a rule connecting questions asked at the surface with answers received there.

Why this is more than bookkeeping The boundary map is obtained only after the exterior field equations and the outgoing condition have been solved. It therefore contains physical information about the surrounding region, not merely a label attached to the surface.

From boundary response to inertial response

If the boundary pattern changes slowly in time, the response changes with it. The part of the response that grows with the square of the frequency behaves like an inertial term: it measures a delayed or reactive resistance to change. By looking only at the simplest directional patterns—the dipole patterns corresponding roughly to the three spatial directions—one obtains a small tensor that plays the role of a laboratory inertial response.

This is reactive inertia. The boundary itself is kept fixed while the field is gently driven. We learn how the exterior stores and returns energy through that stationary surface.

There is, however, another familiar meaning of inertia. We can move or accelerate the laboratory and ask what force is required. The coefficient connecting acceleration with force is translational inertia.

These are not automatically the same experiment:

  • Reactive protocol: keep the boundary in place and vary the field slowly.
  • Translational protocol: move the boundary and calculate the force needed to accelerate it.

The first probes the response of a fixed screen. The second changes the position of the screen itself. It is therefore important not to declare their equality by definition.

Why vacuum makes one inertia look unique

In the solvable scalar vacuum model, the two protocols are controlled by the same dipole response channel. The reactive measurement reads one part of that channel’s slow-frequency behaviour. The translational measurement reads its static stiffness in a different way.

Because empty space supplies no additional distance scale or environmental structure, the two readings fall into a fixed relation. They are not identical measurements, but they are locked together. Knowing one determines the other.

In this model, the apparent uniqueness of inertial mass in vacuum is a result, not an assumption: two distinct experiments are forced into a fixed relation by one shared response channel.

This point changes the interpretation. “There is one inertia” no longer means that every operational definition was secretly the same from the beginning. It means that a simple vacuum environment does not contain enough structure to make the definitions disagree.

What matter outside the boundary changes

Now place a thin shell in the exterior. The boundary response changes because waves or static fields can travel outward, interact with the shell, and return with a modified pattern. The shell adds a new length scale: its distance from the laboratory.

The two inertial protocols then respond differently. The translational measurement is mainly tied to the static dipole stiffness. The reactive measurement contains an additional dependence on how far away the shell is. In the model, their departure from the vacuum relation can therefore be used to infer the shell’s radius.

This is a striking reversal of the usual Machian story. Normally one says that distant matter influences local inertia. Here the pair of local inertial measurements also contains information about the distant matter:

The environment affects inertia, but the mismatch between two kinds of inertia can also act as a probe of the environment.

The result is particularly clean because the vacuum contribution cancels from the mismatch. Empty space provides the baseline locking relation; environmental structure produces the deviation. In the one-shell scalar model, the deviation is a genuinely relational quantity: it vanishes in vacuum and is nonzero because of the system’s relation to its surroundings.

Inertia is real, but indexed by a boundary

Another issue is the choice of coordinates or timing convention on the surface. A raw response coefficient can change when we alter the boundary frame. The model supplies a definite correction, built from lower-order static response, that removes this descriptive dependence. After that correction, the remaining inertial response is independent of the chosen boundary frame.

It still depends on which surface we selected. A larger or differently shaped boundary defines a different division between system and environment. The present result therefore supports a middle position:

  • the response is not merely a coordinate artifact;
  • it is not an absolute property detached from every surroundings;
  • it is a well-defined physical quantity for a specified finite partition.

One might call this finite-boundary relationalism: inertia is real after we remove frame conventions, but it remains tied to the boundary that defines what counts as the laboratory and what counts as its environment.

What has actually been shown

The demonstrated results belong to a scalar field model, chosen because it can be solved exactly enough to expose the structure. They show that a finite boundary response can define a reactive inertial quantity; that a separate moving-boundary protocol defines translational inertia; that the two lock in vacuum; and that a simple shell environment unlocks them in an informative way.

This is not yet a complete theory of gravitational inertia. A scalar field has none of the full gauge structure and tensor dynamics of general relativity. The model should therefore be read as a controlled proof of concept. It identifies a pattern worth testing in gravity, rather than announcing that the origin of inertia has been solved.
