From Local Observation to the Quantum Universe

Every Observation Begins Somewhere

Physics aims to describe the universe as it is. Yet every experiment begins from a particular place within that universe. Every observer occupies one location, performs one measurement, and receives only the information that can physically reach them. Observation is therefore never detached from the world it seeks to describe. It is itself a physical process.

This simple fact raises a surprisingly deep question. When we speak about the universe, are we describing the world itself, or only that part of it which has become accessible through observation? Modern physics suggests that these two viewpoints, although closely related, need not always coincide.

The Observable Universe

Astronomers often speak about the observable universe. This is not the entire universe, but the region from which light and other physical signals have had enough time to reach us since the beginning of cosmic expansion. Everything we know about distant galaxies, stars and the early universe has arrived through this limited flow of information.

The observable universe therefore illustrates a general principle. Knowledge is constrained not only by the laws of physics, but also by the physical pathways through which information becomes available. What cannot reach us cannot become part of observation.

Local Observers and Global Descriptions

Quantum theory introduces a second perspective. Instead of beginning with observations, one may begin with a mathematical description of the entire physical system. As larger and larger systems are included, this description eventually extends to the universe as a whole. Physicists often refer to this as a global quantum state.

These two viewpoints are complementary. One begins with what can be observed. The other begins with what the theory assumes to exist. Neither automatically determines the other. A complete description generally contains more information than any single observer can ever access.

From Global to Local

One direction is straightforward. Starting from a global description, we may ask what information remains accessible to a particular observer. This process does not change the physics. It simply reflects that every observer has access to only part of the whole.

The opposite direction is far more subtle. Suppose we gather together everything that can, in principle, be observed. Can this observable information always be extended to a unique global description of the universe? Modern mathematical physics shows that, in general, the answer is no. Several different global descriptions may remain compatible with exactly the same observable information.

Why This Matters

This observation changes the way we compare physical theories. Two mathematical descriptions may differ in many respects while remaining indistinguishable to every possible experiment available to a particular observer. Conversely, apparently small differences in observable information may reflect profound differences in the underlying global description.

The mathematical work behind these essays develops a framework for studying this relationship. Rather than treating observation as something external to physics, it asks how observable descriptions emerge from the structure of the theory itself. The goal is to understand not only what exists, but what can actually become known.

This question forms one half of the broader programme presented on this website. The companion essays approach the complementary problem: not what an observer can know about the universe, but what distinctions the universe itself is capable of preserving. Together these two questions explore the meeting point between observation and physical reality.
