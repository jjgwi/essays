Two Different Questions

There are two very different questions one can ask about the universe, and they are usually kept apart.

The first looks outward and backward, at the whole and its beginning. What state describes the universe? Why is it the way it is, and not otherwise? These are the traditional questions of cosmology and of the physics of the elementary forces.

The second looks inward, at us. What can we actually know about this universe? Which traces of its history are still accessible today, and which have disappeared forever? These are questions about observation, measurement and the interpretation of data.

At first glance the two have little to do with each other. One concerns what exists. The other concerns what can be observed. Yet they cannot truly be separated. Between the universe itself and every cosmological observation lies an interface. It is here that a tiny part of the universe becomes empirically accessible. Out of the immense richness of the whole emerges a stable collection of quantities that can be prepared, measured and compared.

The Observable Universe

The previous essays argued that every observation takes place through an interface. Cosmology makes this idea unavoidable. Unlike laboratory physics, there is no external observer who can step outside the experiment. The observable universe is not one subsystem among many. It is the complete empirical interface available to us. Everything we know about the cosmos reaches us through it.

At first sight this seems to settle the matter. If all cosmological knowledge comes through an interface, perhaps cosmology itself should begin there. The observable quantities available to us would then provide the natural foundation from which to understand the universe.

The Global State

Modern quantum cosmology often follows a different path. Its starting point is not observation but quantum theory itself.

Suppose a quantum system is prepared in a superposition of several possible outcomes. One way to describe a measurement is to assume that the wavefunction collapses and one outcome becomes reality. Another is to include the measuring apparatus in the quantum description. The system and apparatus then form a larger quantum system described by a joint quantum state.

Nothing prevents continuing this reasoning. The laboratory may itself be included. So may the observer. With every interaction the quantum description grows until, in principle, it encompasses the entire universe. This leads naturally to the idea of a single global quantum state, often called the wavefunction of the universe.

We therefore arrive at two very different starting points. One begins with what can be observed. The other begins with a description of the universe as a whole. Neither viewpoint is obviously more fundamental than the other.

Connecting the Two Pictures

Rather than choosing between these viewpoints, one may instead ask how they are related. Can a global quantum description and an observable description be understood within a common framework?

One direction is conceptually straightforward. If a global description of the universe is given, only part of its content can ever become observable. Passing from the complete description to the observable one is called a restriction. Restriction does not destroy information. It simply reflects the fact that every observation is limited to the quantities accessible through our interface.

The opposite direction is more subtle. Suppose we combine everything that can, in principle, be observed from all available observational viewpoints. In algebraic quantum field theory this collection is represented by a joint observable algebra. Can such an observable structure be completed to a global quantum description? This is the problem of extension.

Unlike restriction, extension is generally not unique. Several different global descriptions may remain compatible with exactly the same observable structure. Restriction and extension therefore do not simply undo one another. Instead they reveal a genuine tension between observable physics and global descriptions of the universe.

Why This Matters

This tension is not merely philosophical. It has practical consequences for cosmology. Different theories of the early universe often propose different global quantum states. The observable universe, however, provides access only to restricted information. Understanding how observable structures relate to possible global descriptions therefore becomes essential when asking whether two cosmological models are genuinely distinguishable by observation.

The framework developed in these essays does not assume that either the observable or the global viewpoint is primary. Instead, it provides a language in which the relationship between them can be explored. Cosmology can be approached from two directions: from what can be observed, or from what is assumed to exist. Restriction and extension provide a way of relating these perspectives without assuming that either one is fundamental.

Which Differences Remain Observable?

If several different global descriptions remain compatible with the same observable universe, an important question immediately follows. Which differences between these descriptions could ever become observable, and which remain forever hidden?

Imagine two complete descriptions of the universe. They may differ in many details, but if every experiment available to a bounded observer produces the same results, those differences have no operational meaning for that observer. From the observational point of view, the two descriptions are equivalent.

This shift in emphasis is important. The central problem is not merely how to discard information, but how to identify the information that genuinely belongs to a given observer. Once this viewpoint is adopted, these two questions that seem unrelated—from laboratory physics to cosmology—can be discussed within the same conceptual framework.

When physicists try to apply quantum mechanics to the entire universe (quantum cosmology), they write down mathematical equations to describe everything that exists. They describe the universe in a superposition of different states; different possible universes.

The question that can be naturally posed in the framework is one that unifies that "Two different questions": whether an observation or experiment in our model ever actually can tell us the difference between these two mathematical states of the two possible universes. If an experiment can eventually prove a difference exists, the differente is real. If no experiment in the universe can ever detect it, then the two states are effectively the same in practice. You can still argue about it philosophically, but science can't tell them apart.
