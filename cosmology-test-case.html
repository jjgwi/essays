From Observable Physics to the Universe

The previous essay introduced two complementary ways of describing the same physical world. One begins with what can be observed. The other begins with a larger mathematical description from which observable physics can be recovered. Although these viewpoints are closely related, they are not identical. Observable information does not necessarily determine a unique global description.

Imagine looking at a city through a small window. Everything visible through the window is perfectly real, yet many different cities might produce exactly the same view. Observation therefore constrains the larger picture without uniquely determining it.

Modern quantum theory leads to a similar situation. A local observer has access only to observable quantities. Several different global quantum descriptions may nevertheless remain compatible with exactly the same observable physics.

When Differences Matter

This immediately raises a deeper question. Suppose two global descriptions are not identical. Should we automatically regard that difference as physically important? Not necessarily. Some differences lead to clearly distinguishable observable predictions. Others remain hidden from every experiment available to the observer. Still others disappear entirely once attention is restricted to observable physics.

The important question is therefore no longer simply whether two mathematical descriptions differ. It is whether those differences survive when translated into observable predictions.

Why Cosmology?

Cosmology provides perhaps the clearest example of this problem. We observe only one universe and therefore only one realized cosmic history. Unlike laboratory physics, we cannot repeat the evolution of the universe under slightly different conditions, nor can we compare our universe with alternative realizations.

Quantum cosmology nevertheless describes a much richer mathematical structure. Different global descriptions naturally give rise to different possible histories of the universe. Although only one of these histories is ultimately observed, many neighbouring alternatives remain part of the theoretical description.

This changes the scientific problem completely. Instead of asking only whether two possible histories differ, we must ask a more subtle question. If two competing descriptions are different at the mathematical level, how much of that original difference survives once both are translated into physical predictions?

This is not merely a question about cosmology. It is a general question about scientific theories. Some competing hypotheses remain easily distinguishable because their predictions differ dramatically. Others become almost impossible to tell apart because their original differences largely disappear when confronted with observation. Understanding this relationship between theoretical differences and observable differences is one of the central motivations behind the mathematical work described on this website.

A Question Before a Definition

The mathematical notes introduce a precise way of comparing how much of the distinction between two competing descriptions survives into their observable predictions. Eventually this comparison will be formalized by a quantity called Reach.

Rather than defining Reach immediately, however, it is helpful to understand why such a comparison is needed in the first place. Remarkably, one of the clearest examples comes not from cosmology, but from biology. More than a century after Charles Darwin first recognized the importance of variation within populations, Peter and Rosemary Grant watched nature perform precisely this kind of comparison among the finches of the Galápagos Islands.

Their observations provide an unexpectedly clear physical intuition for the problem we wish to study. Suppose two competing explanations differ only slightly. How much of that original difference survives once nature itself acts upon the population? Answering this question in biology will prepare us to ask the corresponding question again in quantum cosmology.
