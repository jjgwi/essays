Which differences are observable?

When two candidate wavefunctions of the universe are distinguishable to us at all

The starting point

For the early universe there are several proposals for which quantum state describes its beginning. Two well-known ones carry the names of their authors: the Hartle–Hawking proposal and the tunnelling proposal. Both are serious candidates, and for decades it has been debated which is the right one.

The obvious question would be: are these two states different at all? This note asks a different, more precise question. For even if two proposals differ mathematically, it does not yet follow that we can ever measure the difference. The real question is therefore:

Which differences between two candidate wavefunctions survive the long path down to the quantities we can actually observe today — and which are lost along the way?

Why some differences are lost

The reason lies in the way we observe the universe. We have no access to the complete quantum state. What reaches us is a few measurable quantities — the strength of the density fluctuations in the microwave background, their distribution across different scales, or the curvature of space.

The path from the original state to these measured quantities is a kind of filter. Each proposal distributes its “weight” over different possible histories of the universe, and this filter maps all those histories onto the few observable numbers. The decisive observation is: this filter is not invertible. Different prehistories can lead to exactly the same measured values.

The criterion

This is where the actual result of this note lies, and it takes the form of a clear criterion. It can be put roughly like this:

If an observable quantity has the same value across a whole range of possible prehistories, then it is blind to how two proposals distribute their weight within that range.

Put differently: if two proposals differ only where the observable is constant anyway, then they yield exactly the same prediction — the difference is invisible to that measurement, not because it does not exist, but because the observation does not resolve it. It becomes visible only once one adds a further quantity that is not constant in that region.

This criterion is the robust, generally valid core. For any observable quantity it says whether or not it can distinguish two proposals — independently of which concrete model one considers.

A concrete suspicion — cautiously stated

In a simple, worked-through model case a striking pattern appears: part of the standard observables of cosmology — the strength and distribution of the density fluctuations, say — seems to be “saturated” over broad ranges, that is, constant and hence blind to the difference between the proposals. A single quantity, on the other hand, the residual curvature of space, might remain sensitive within a bounded window.

That would be, if it holds up, a remarkably sharp picture: the most-measured quantities of cosmology would then carry no information at all about the choice between the two proposals, and a single quantity would carry it. But I state this point deliberately only as a suspicion and as a target for a more careful calculation — not as an established result. It depends on the assumptions of the particular model. What is robust is the criterion of the previous section; which quantity carries the difference in the actual universe is not thereby settled.

What this means

The dispute “Hartle–Hawking or tunnelling?” thereby turns into a sharper and answerable question. Instead of asking whether the two states are different, one asks: at which point, in which measured quantity, does their difference survive the path down to us? Where the answer is “nowhere,” the two proposals are — for these observations — not competing physical theories but indistinguishable. Where it is “here,” one has identified a genuine, measurable prediction.

This is not a new cosmological model and not a resolution of the old dispute. It is a tool that sorts the dispute: it separates the differences that can become observational data from those that in principle cannot.
