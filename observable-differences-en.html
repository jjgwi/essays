Which differences are observable?

How a physical interface turns a global quantum state into a limited set of records

A difference on paper is not yet a difference in nature

Cosmologists have proposed several quantum descriptions of the universe’s beginning. Two famous examples are the Hartle–Hawking proposal and the tunnelling proposal. They assign different weights to possible early histories of the cosmos and are therefore different mathematical objects.

But that is not yet enough to make them different physical theories for us. A difference matters observationally only if it can travel through cosmic evolution and appear in something that can be measured: a pattern of galaxies, a feature of the cosmic microwave background, a trace of spatial curvature, or another durable record.

The important question is not only whether two global wavefunctions differ, but whether any physically available observation can tell them apart.

An interface acts like a filter

A physical interface never reads the complete quantum state of the universe. It responds only to certain features. A camera records light that reaches its sensor, not every event outside its field of view. A radio receiver selects a narrow band of frequencies. A cosmological observation is similar: it samples a limited set of quantities after billions of years of evolution.

We can therefore think of an interface as a filter. Many different global states can pass through the filter and produce the same output. They may differ in details to which the interface is blind—in phases, remote correlations, rapidly decaying modes, or features that never became stable records.

All global states that give the same interface data form an observational family. From the standpoint of the present records, they are equivalent. This does not say that they are identical everywhere. It says that their differences have no representative in the chosen observations.

Records are selected by dynamics

We should not choose the filter arbitrarily. The universe itself helps determine which quantities become records. A candidate variable must couple to something, produce a response, and remain stable long enough to be read. Variables that are amplified, copied, or frozen into matter become accessible. Variables whose influence rapidly fades are much harder to recover.

In the early universe, quantum fluctuations were stretched to enormous scales. For many cosmological modes, one combination of the original quantum variables became nearly constant, while another combination decayed. The surviving combination later influenced the distribution of matter and the temperature pattern of the cosmic microwave background. It became the dominant record.

This process is often described as cosmological squeezing. The word can sound mysterious, but the basic idea is simple: evolution makes one direction in the quantum state easy to observe and its partner increasingly difficult to detect.

Not an absolute erasure At any finite time the suppressed direction may still leave a tiny imprint. The distinction is therefore often one of degree, not a perfect yes-or-no division. In an ideal limit the imprint disappears; in the real universe it may merely fall below the sensitivity of the available records.

Amplitude is easier to keep than phase

A simple wave has both an amplitude and a phase. The amplitude tells us how large a variation is. The phase helps tell us where the wave sits in its cycle and how it interferes with other possibilities. Quantum states also carry amplitude and phase information, though in a richer form.

Many cosmological records are mainly sensitive to the distribution of amplitudes. They tell us how likely different fluctuation strengths were and how those strengths varied with scale. They may be far less sensitive to the original quantum phase. Two wavefunctions can therefore have the same amplitude distribution and different phases, yet agree on all measurements made by an amplitude-only interface.

At finite squeezing, some weak phase sensitivity can remain because the recorded quantity is not perfectly aligned with amplitude alone. This is important. It prevents us from claiming that phase information is fundamentally meaningless. The careful statement is that a given physical interface may retain it only weakly—or not at all in an idealized limit.

The saturation test

The main criterion can be stated without equations. Imagine that many possible early histories lead to exactly the same value of an observable. Across that range, the observable has saturated: it no longer changes when the early history changes.

If two proposals differ only in how they distribute weight among histories that produce the same observable result, that observable cannot distinguish them.

To reveal the difference, we need another quantity that still varies across those histories. The first observable was not measured badly; it simply did not carry the missing information. No improvement in data analysis can recover a distinction that the physical channel failed to preserve.

This is a useful discipline in cosmology. It prevents us from treating every mathematical difference between proposals as a future observation waiting to happen. Some differences may be compressed into the same data long before any telescope is built.

More observations refine the picture

Adding compatible records can separate states that previously looked identical. Suppose two early-universe proposals predict the same spectrum of density fluctuations but different residual curvature. Measurements of the fluctuation spectrum alone would place them in one observational family. A sufficiently precise curvature measurement might split that family into two.

Additional observations can therefore narrow the range of global states compatible with the data. They cannot, however, make already distinguished states become identical. In this sense, scientific progress refines our observational classes.

The strongest possible interface would preserve enough information to reconstruct every state in the particular family of proposals under study. Usually we have something weaker: partial distinguishability. We can tell some proposals apart in some directions, while other differences remain hidden.

A cautious application to the beginning of the universe

To compare the Hartle–Hawking and tunnelling proposals fairly, both must be passed through the same chain: the same model of the early universe, the same laws of evolution, and the same account of how records form. Only then should their predictions be compared.

Simple model calculations suggest an intriguing possibility. Familiar observables such as the overall strength and scale dependence of primordial fluctuations may become nearly insensitive across broad ranges of early histories. Residual spatial curvature may remain sensitive in a narrower window. If so, curvature would carry information that the standard fluctuation observables have lost.

This is a research target, not a settled result about our universe. It depends on the model used, the choice of boundary conditions, the measure assigned to histories, and the precision of the realized observations. The robust conclusion is the method: first identify the physical record channel, then test which differences it preserves.

Hidden now, visible later?

A difference that is invisible to one interface need not be invisible forever. A later stage of evolution may couple it into a new record. A different detector may be sensitive to another combination of variables. Some information can also remain present in global correlations even when no local measurement can recover it easily.

There are several possibilities:

  • a difference is invisible to current observations but could appear in a future channel;
  • it produces a very small signal that is present but practically undetectable;
  • it is lost to every interface that can physically form;
  • it is not a physical difference at all, but only a change of mathematical description.

Distinguishing these cases is essential. “We cannot observe it now” does not imply “it does not exist.” But “the equations look different” does not imply “nature is different,” either.

A connection to holography and ER = EPR

Modern quantum gravity repeatedly asks how much of a larger world can be reconstructed from limited access. In holography, data associated with part of a boundary can sometimes determine a corresponding region of the interior. In black-hole physics, quantum correlations may be tied to the connectivity of spacetime itself. The conjecture ER = EPR is the boldest expression of that possibility.

The interface viewpoint fits naturally beside these ideas because it also studies reconstruction from partial quantum data. Yet it does not prove that entanglement is a wormhole, or that every hidden correlation has a geometric meaning. The reliable connection is structural: in each case, access to a restricted set of observables determines some global features while leaving others unresolved.

What the criterion gives us

The framework does not choose the correct wavefunction of the universe. It does not promise that every global difference will eventually become measurable. It gives something more modest and more useful: a way to audit a proposed distinction.

Which physical record carries the difference? How strongly does it carry it? Could a richer interface recover more? Or did cosmic evolution map several global possibilities onto the same observable world?

Those questions turn a vague debate about competing wavefunctions into a sequence of testable problems. They tell us where to look—and, just as importantly, when a difference in the theory may leave no difference in the data.
