One Universe, Many Possible Histories

Modern cosmology differs from almost every other branch of physics. A laboratory experiment can be repeated under slightly different conditions. The universe cannot. We observe one universe, one cosmic history, and one realization of everything that has happened since the Big Bang. Yet quantum cosmology naturally describes something richer than this single history. Instead of one predetermined evolution, the mathematical framework contains many alternative branch histories that are compatible with the underlying laws of physics. The observable universe corresponds to one realized branch, but the theory contains many neighbouring possibilities.

Bottom-Up and Top-Down Questions

Historically, cosmology has often adopted a bottom-up perspective. One specifies an initial quantum state together with the dynamical laws and asks what universe follows. James Hartle and Stephen Hawking suggested that cosmology can also be viewed from the opposite direction. Instead of asking how the universe evolved from its beginning, one may begin with today's observations and ask which branch histories are compatible with them. This top-down viewpoint shifts the emphasis from predicting one history to understanding an entire family of possible histories.

The change appears subtle, but it has profound consequences. Once many histories become part of the description, an immediate question arises. How should those histories contribute to the probabilities of the observations we actually make?

Histories Are Not Yet Predictions

Simply listing all admissible histories is not enough. A collection of histories does not by itself tell us what an observer should expect to see. A further ingredient is required. Some principle must relate the ensemble of possible histories to the probabilities assigned to observations. In other words, the theory must explain how predictions emerge from histories.

Different Ways of Assigning Probabilities

Modern quantum cosmology contains several proposals for performing this step. Some approaches give greater statistical weight to histories occupying larger physical volumes. Others average over observations rather than over space. Still others introduce additional observational conditions before assigning probabilities. Although these proposals differ in detail, they all address the same conceptual question: How should possible histories contribute to the probabilities of what is observed?

The important point is that these proposals begin with the same collection of possible histories. What changes is the rule by which those histories contribute to observational predictions. The histories remain unchanged. The probabilities do not.

A Simple Illustration

Imagine two cosmologists studying exactly the same family of branch histories. The first assigns greater importance to histories containing larger spatial volumes. The second instead averages over observations without favouring larger universes. Neither cosmologist changes the histories themselves. Only the method used to translate histories into observational probabilities is different. Yet the resulting predictions may differ substantially. The scientific question is therefore no longer simply which histories exist. It becomes how different weighting principles shape the predictions derived from those histories.

Why Comparison Becomes Necessary

Once several physically reasonable weighting principles exist, another question naturally follows. How should they be compared? This is not unique to cosmology. Scientists routinely compare competing explanations that begin from the same physical system but organize it in different ways. The previous essays illustrated this idea through Darwin's finches. Different ecological models acted upon the same population and produced different expectations regarding survival. Quantum cosmology presents an analogous situation. Different conditioning proposals act upon the same branching structure while leading to different observational predictions.

From Scientific Debate to Mathematical Language

The accompanying mathematical framework does not attempt to replace these physical ideas. Instead, it provides a common language in which they can be compared. Each conditioning proposal determines a family of observational predictions. Those predictions occupy a region within the larger space of all possible branch distributions. Some proposals can realize many different observational patterns. Others are much more constrained. The mathematical concepts introduced in the accompanying notes—including reachable sets, capacity, concentration and Reach—arise from analysing these regions and comparing them systematically.

A Different Way of Looking at Cosmology

The central idea is therefore not to propose yet another cosmological model. It is to change the way competing cosmological proposals are compared. Instead of asking only whether two conditioning principles are mathematically different, we ask how differently they organize the same family of possible histories and how much of those differences survive in the predictions they produce. This shift—from comparing histories to comparing the ways histories are turned into observations—is the conceptual starting point of the mathematical framework developed in the accompanying paper.
